What this actually means

Start from a blueprint, not from a prompt

Ask a general-purpose model for ten questions on a chapter and you get ten questions on a chapter: same difficulty, same shape, clustered on whatever the source text emphasised. An assessment is a specification before it is a set of questions.

You set the coverage

Which concepts the paper must test and how many marks each is worth. Generation fills the blueprint rather than deciding what matters, so the paper matches what you taught.

You set the difficulty spread

A paper of uniformly moderate questions separates nobody. Specify the mix across recall, application and analysis and the generated set is built to it.

Every item is tagged

Each question carries the concept it tests and the level it targets. That tagging is what turns a mark into a diagnosis afterwards, and it is why the results are usable.

The tagging is the quiet part that matters most. A paper of untagged questions produces a number; a paper of tagged questions produces a list of concepts the class has not understood. See what tagged results make possible.

Item quality

Six ways a generated question goes wrong

Anyone who has reviewed machine-written questions has met all of these. They are worth naming, because a tool that does not check for them is asking the teacher to be the entire quality process.

  • Fault 01

    Distractors nobody would pick

    Three wrong options that are obviously wrong turn a four-option question into a true-or-false. Each distractor should correspond to a specific mistake a learner actually makes.

  • Fault 02

    More than one defensible answer

    The most common serious fault and the hardest to spot at speed. Candidate items are checked for it and flagged to the reviewer rather than quietly published.

  • Fault 03

    Testing the phrasing, not the concept

    A question answerable by recognising wording lifted from the textbook measures reading memory. Items are written to require the concept rather than the sentence.

  • Fault 04

    An ambiguous stem

    If a capable student has to guess what is being asked, the item measures inference. Stems are checked for the vagueness that only shows up under exam pressure.

  • Fault 05

    Difficulty drift

    Generated sets tend to converge on the middle. The blueprint is enforced across the whole paper, not item by item, so the spread you asked for survives.

  • Fault 06

    Answers hidden in the options

    Length, grammatical agreement and specificity all leak the correct answer to a test-wise student. These patterns are checked before an item reaches review.

The workflow

Nothing reaches a student unreviewed

Speed is only worth having if the output is safe to use. The path from a blueprint to a published paper has a person in it, and the platform is built so that person's job is reviewing rather than rewriting.

  1. Specify. Concepts, marks, difficulty mix and question types, drawn from the syllabus already loaded.
  2. Generate. Candidate items produced against your own course material rather than the open internet.
  3. Screen. Automated checks for the six faults above. Suspect items arrive flagged, with the reason stated.
  4. Review. The teacher accepts, edits or rejects each item. Rejections are cheap; a replacement is one click.
  5. Publish. Approved items go to the class and into the bank, attributed to the person who approved them.
  6. Learn from the results. Items everyone answers correctly, or nobody does, are surfaced for retirement next time.
What the AI will not do

The same tool, three jobs

Schools
Weekly class tests and practice sets against the subject syllabus, generated in a free period rather than an evening. For schools.
Higher Education
Unit tests and question banks per course, tagged to outcomes so results feed department reporting. AI for faculty.
Enterprise
Knowledge checks inside a skill assessment, written against the level definitions a role standard uses. Skill assessment.

Questions

What teachers and faculty ask

Are the generated questions accurate?

Mostly, and not reliably enough to publish unreviewed, which is why the workflow does not allow it. Grounding generation in your own course material removes a large class of errors, and the automated screening catches several more. What remains is a review step measured in minutes rather than the hours it takes to write a paper from scratch.

Can it produce anything other than multiple choice?

Yes. Short answer, numerical, matching, true or false and longer written prompts with a marking rubric attached. Multiple choice gets the most attention because it is the format where item quality fails most quietly, but a paper built only from one format measures the format as much as the subject.

Does every student get the same paper?

Your choice per assessment. For a formal test, one fixed paper is usually what an institution wants. For practice, generating a different set per student from the same blueprint is more useful, because it makes the practice genuinely individual and makes copying pointless.

Can it grade the results as well?

Objective items are marked automatically, and written answers can be given draft feedback against the rubric. Final marks on formal or high-stakes assessments stay with the examiner. That boundary is deliberate: marking carries academic and regulatory weight that should not sit with a model.
