Employee skill assessment

Measure What Your Employees Can Actually Do

Most skills data inside an organization is a survey: people rated themselves, a manager nodded, and a spreadsheet was born. Assessment replaces the opinion with evidence, so the level on a person's profile is something they demonstrated.

What this actually means

Establishing a level, not producing a score

A skill assessment answers one narrow question: at what level can this person perform this skill today. Not how they feel about it, not how long they have been in the job, and not whether they finished a course about it two years ago.

The scale

Level 1 — Aware
Knows the concepts and vocabulary. Cannot yet produce work in this skill.
Level 2 — Assisted
Completes routine tasks with guidance and review.
Level 3 — Independent
Handles normal work in this skill without supervision.
Level 4 — Advanced
Handles non-standard and ambiguous cases, and reviews others' work.
Level 5 — Expert
Sets the standard for the skill and develops other people in it.
The definitions are yours
This is a sensible default. Organizations that already run a competency framework keep their own wording and their own number of levels.

What it gives you

An inventory you can make decisions from

  • Stop planning against a skills list nobody believes.

    Levels come from demonstrated evidence, so a workforce view is something a leadership team can plan against rather than politely ignore.

    Evidence-based levels

  • Find the capability you already paid for.

    People routinely hold skills their current role does not use. An inventory built from assessment surfaces them before a role goes out to the market.

    Skills inventory

  • Compare like with like across teams.

    One scale, one set of level definitions, one method. Level 3 means the same thing in two departments, which is what makes any comparison honest.

    Common framework

  • Watch a level move rather than assume it did.

    Re-assessment after learning gives a second measurement on the same skill, so capability change is observed instead of inferred from course completions.

    Re-assessment

  • Keep the measurement short enough that people finish it.

    Assessments are scoped to one skill at a time and mixed in format, so measuring a role's skill set is a series of short sittings rather than an exam day.

    Scoped assessments

  • Give the employee something worth having.

    Each person gets their own profile: where they stand, what evidence supports it, and what the next level would require of them.

A worked example

How one level gets established

Take a single skill — query optimisation — for a single person. Four steps, and the fourth is the one that stops a skills inventory going stale.

  1. 1. Define what each level looks like

    Before anyone is measured, the skill is described at every level in observable terms. If two reviewers would disagree about what Level 3 means, the definition is not finished.

  2. 2. Gather more than one kind of evidence

    A knowledge check, an applied task, and a scenario that has no clean answer. Three formats, because any single format measures itself as much as it measures the skill.

  3. 3. Place the level, and say how sure you are

    The level is placed against the definitions, with a confidence that reflects how much evidence sits behind it. Thin evidence produces a level marked as provisional, not a level presented as fact.

  4. 4. Give it an expiry

    Skills decay and tools change. A level carries the date it was established and a review interval, so a three-year-old measurement is never quietly presented as a current one.

Questions

What HR and L&D ask first

Is this used in performance reviews or promotion decisions?

That is your call, and we would urge caution. A skill level describes capability on one skill at one point in time; it says nothing about contribution, judgement or how someone works with others. The platform gives managers the measurement. It does not rank people and it does not make personnel decisions.

How long does assessing one skill take?

Assessments are scoped to a single skill and usually sit in the range of a short sitting rather than an examination. Measuring an entire role's skill set is spread across several sittings, because a four-hour assessment day produces fatigue data as much as skills data.

Can managers override an assessed level?

A manager can add their own validation, and it is recorded as a separate, attributed piece of evidence rather than silently replacing the assessed result. Where the two disagree, that disagreement is visible, which is usually more useful than either number on its own.

What about skills that cannot be tested with questions?

Plenty cannot. For those, evidence is a submitted work product, a structured scenario, or a manager validation against the level definitions. We would rather record a level as provisional and say what it rests on than pretend a multiple-choice test measured collaboration.
